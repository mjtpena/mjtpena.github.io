Andriel showed me his new Pokémon drawing. I was looking at my phone.

"Dad, look!"

"Mm-hmm, that's great, bud."

I hadn't even looked. He knew. Kids always know.

That exchange has stuck with me. It wasn't a missed school event or a broken promise. It was smaller than that, and that's exactly why it bothers me: it's the kind of moment I'd never notice myself doing unless he made me notice.

## The pattern

I'm physically present but mentally elsewhere. Work problems. Upcoming deadlines. That bug I can't figure out.

My kids get my body. My attention is somewhere else.

Earlier this month I wrote about the bigger version of this problem in [The Tech Parent's Dilemma](/blog/2026-01-06-parenting-in-tech/): working from home put me near my family all day without actually being with them. That post was about boundaries around the workday: shutdown times, calendar blocks, no Slack after hours. Those rules help. But this moment happened outside any meeting. Nobody was paging me. I was just on my phone, and a habit filled the gap where my attention should have been.

Boundaries protect time. They don't automatically produce presence. You can clear an entire Saturday and still spend it half-listening.

## Why "mm-hmm" isn't neutral

It's tempting to think a distracted "that's great" is harmless. At least I responded, right?

I don't think it is. When a child brings you something (a drawing, a story, a question), they're making what relationship researcher John Gottman calls a bid for connection. The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard describes this kind of back-and-forth as ["serve and return"](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/key-concepts/serve-and-return/): the child serves, the adult returns the serve with eye contact, words or attention, and those exchanges matter for development. Their research focuses on early childhood, and Andriel is past that stage, but I don't need a study to tell me he knew. A return without looking isn't really a return. It's a polite way of saying "not now", and kids know it when they hear it.

He's also learning from it. If "Dad, look!" reliably gets a reply to the phone instead of to him, sooner or later he'll stop saying it. The American Academy of Pediatrics makes a similar point in its policy statement [Media and Young Minds](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591), a statement aimed at children under six: heavy parent use of mobile devices is associated with fewer verbal and nonverbal interactions with their children. I'd rather fix my habit than teach him that lesson.

## The change

New rule: when my kids talk to me, the phone goes down. The computer stays closed. I look at them.

That's it. No app, no timer, no system. I like solving things with tooling, but this one doesn't need tooling. It needs me to turn my head.

The reasoning is simple: fully present for five minutes beats half-present for an hour. Most of what my kids bring me doesn't need an hour. A drawing needs a minute of actually looking, a question or two about which Pokémon it is, and some real enthusiasm. A story about their day needs me to stop scrolling until it's finished. Five minutes, given properly, is usually enough, and then everyone gets on with what they were doing.

There are times I genuinely can't stop: a live client call, or driving. Then I say so out loud and give a specific time ("I'm on a call, show me in ten minutes"), and when the time is up I go and ask to see it. A deferral he can trust is better than a fake "that's great".

That's where the title comes from. I'm not aiming to be a father who's available every minute. I'm aiming to be a father who's actually there for the five minutes that count.

## What I'm learning

**Presence doesn't happen by default.** You have to choose it, every time, and the phone makes not choosing it very easy.

**Kids know the difference** between being in the room and being *there*. Andriel didn't need me to explain that I was distracted. He could see it.

**Five focused minutes beat an hour of half-listening.** That's not an excuse to give my kids less time; it's a reminder that time I spend not paying attention barely counts.

**Tomorrow isn't guaranteed.** I might not get another chance to see this drawing, hear this story, share this moment. Kids don't repeat their bids on a schedule that suits me.

## The reality check

I'm not perfect at this. I still get distracted. I still think about work.

But I'm trying. The rule is deliberately small so I can't argue my way out of it. "Be more present" is a resolution I'd quietly abandon. "Phone down when they talk" is a rule I can keep, and catch myself breaking. It's the same approach I described in [New Year, Same Foundation](/blog/2026-01-01-new-year-same-foundation/): small things I can actually do, rather than grand promises I won't.

If you're a parent who spends your day on a screen, try the same rule for a week. Don't measure anything. Just notice how often you'd have answered your kid without looking up.

## To my kids

I'm sorry for the moments I was there but not really there.

I'm working on it.

You deserve my full attention. I'm learning to give it.

I love you. When I'm with you, I want to truly be *with* you. That's the goal I'm aiming for.
