Another year begins, and I find myself less excited about "new year, new me" proclamations. Maybe it's age, or maybe it's finally understanding that real growth doesn't reset with the calendar. The person who went to bed on 31 December is the same person who wakes up on 1 January, and any plan that ignores that starts from a false premise.

## The resolution trap

I stopped making New Year's resolutions a few years back. Not because I don't believe in self-improvement, but because I realised I was setting myself up for failure by treating 1 January as a magical reset button.

The problem with a resolution is its shape. It's usually a big, binary promise ("get fit", "write a book", "learn a new language") made at the moment of maximum optimism and minimum information. The first missed day feels like the whole thing has failed, so it gets quietly abandoned by February. That isn't a willpower problem. It's a design problem.

What does change from year to year is my awareness. Each year I become a little more conscious of my patterns, my strengths, and my limitations. I'm learning to work with who I am rather than against it, and that's a far better starting point than pretending to be someone else for a few weeks.

## Building on what's already there

This year, I'm not starting from zero. Nobody is. I'm building on:

- The parenting lessons learned from Andriel and Archael
- The technical expertise accumulated over years of working with Azure and AI
- The relationships I've nurtured in the tech community
- The habits, good and bad, that define my daily routine

That last one matters most. The bad habits are part of the foundation too, and pretending they don't exist is how resolutions fail. I'd rather name them and work around them than declare them gone. My rule of thumb: if a habit keeps winning, change the environment rather than relying on willpower. Charge the phone outside the bedroom, pack the gym bag the night before. Making the good option the easy one does more than any promise made at midnight.

So instead of revolutionary change, I'm looking at incremental improvement. Can I be 1% better as a father? As a developer? As a husband? Small gains are boring to announce and easy to keep, and they compound over time in a way that grand gestures never do.

## What I'm carrying forward

These are principles I've already been living by, imperfectly, and want to keep. None of them started on 1 January.

**Consistency over intensity.** I'd rather write 300 words daily than promise myself a book and write nothing. A small thing done every day beats a big thing planned forever. The same is true of exercise, learning, and showing up for people.

**Depth over breadth.** I'm done chasing every new AI model or framework. There's a new model or agent framework most weeks, and trying to keep up with all of it leaves you knowing a little about everything and not enough about anything to ship it well. I want to master what I use rather than dabble in everything. When something new genuinely changes how I'd build a solution, I'll learn it properly. My test is simple: would I use it on a client project in the next six months? If not, it goes on a list, not into my evenings.

**Presence over productivity.** My kids won't remember how many pull requests I merged. They'll remember if I was there. Productivity is easy to measure, which is exactly why it tends to crowd out the things that aren't.

## The AI reality check

We're entering 2026 with models that write, reason over documents and call tools well enough to put into production, which three years ago was still a demo. But I'm not caught up in the hype anymore. The tools are impressive, but they're still just tools. The thinking, the strategy, the human judgement: that's still on us.

The trap I want to avoid is confusing access to a capable model with having solved a problem. Anyone can call an API. Knowing which problem is worth solving, what "good" looks like, and when the answer is "don't use AI for this" is where the actual work sits.

I'm particularly interested in how AI agents will evolve this year, but I'm approaching them with measured expectations. Real value comes from thoughtful implementation, not just deployment. ## How I'll judge the year

Resolutions are measured on 1 January by how ambitious they sound. I'd rather measure the year on 31 December by how it actually went. At the end of 2026, I'll ask myself four questions:

- Did I show up consistently for my family?
- Did I create value for my clients and community?
- Did I maintain my mental and physical health?
- Did I learn something meaningful?

None of these has a number attached, and that's deliberate. Each is something I can check against the year as I lived it: the weekends, the work I delivered, the health habits I kept or let slip, the things I can now do that I couldn't in January. If the honest answer to all four is yes, it was a good year, regardless of what happened to my calendar, my inbox, or the latest model leaderboard. The rest is noise.

If you're tempted to write a list of resolutions today, try a different exercise: write down what you're already doing well and want to keep. That list is your foundation, and the same exercise works for technical skills, which I've covered in [setting technical goals that actually work](/blog/2025-12-31-december-ai-topic/). Build on it. Happy 2026.
