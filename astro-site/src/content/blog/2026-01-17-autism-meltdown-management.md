A meltdown isn't a tantrum, and treating it like one makes everything worse. A tantrum usually has a goal; a meltdown is what happens when our son is overwhelmed and has run out of ways to cope. The [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences) describes it as an intense response to an overwhelming situation, where the person temporarily loses control of their behaviour, and that matches what we see.

This is the short list of what we've learned. It isn't clinical advice, and every autistic child is different, so what works for him won't map neatly onto every family. It's what works and doesn't work in our house.

## What doesn't help

Most of the instincts you'd bring to a tantrum backfire here.

- **"Calm down."** If he could calm down, he would. Saying it only adds pressure to a nervous system that's already overloaded, and it makes things worse.
- **Reasoning mid-meltdown.** Explanations, logic and negotiation all need a brain that has capacity left, and in the middle of a meltdown there isn't any. He can't process words at that point, however sensible they are.
- **Punishment.** Punishing a meltdown is punishing distress, because it isn't a tantrum. It teaches nothing except that being overwhelmed gets you in trouble.
- **Worrying about public embarrassment.** His need comes before other people's judgement: whatever strangers think, the meltdown is far worse for him than it is for us.

## What does help

**Remove triggers.** Leave the situation when possible. If something in the environment caused it, staying there keeps feeding it.

**Reduce stimulation.** Quiet, dim, calm. Less noise, less light, fewer people, fewer words.

**Physical comfort.** If he allows it, gentle pressure or holding. The "if he allows it" matters. Touch that helps one moment can make things worse the next, so we follow his lead.

**Wait it out.** Meltdowns end when they end. They can't be rushed. In the moment, our job is to keep everyone safe and the space calm.

**Recover afterwards.** Talk about it later, when he's calm. That's when he can actually take something in. "Later" means once he has properly settled, not the minute the visible part stops, because the overload doesn't switch off that quickly. The conversation isn't a lecture: it's about what happened just before, and what might make it easier next time. That's also when we can work out what set it off.

## Prevention strategies

We can't prevent every meltdown, but we can stop some of them from starting.

**Warn about changes.** "In 5 minutes we're leaving." Transitions without warning are hard for him. I wrote more about why routine and advance notice matter so much to him in [What My Son's Autism Taught Me About Predictable Systems](/blog/2026-01-08-autism-and-routine/).

**Avoid known triggers.** Loud places during busy times are the obvious one for us.

**Watch for early signs.** Hands over ears, increased stimming, verbal withdrawal. These are the signals that he's heading towards overload. The [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences) also points to signs of distress that often come before a meltdown, and they differ from child to child, which is why we learned his rather than relying on a generic list. Spotting them early gives us a chance to step in before the meltdown starts, rather than managing it once it has.

**Have exit strategies.** Always know how to leave quickly. In practice that means knowing, before we need it, where the way out is and where a quieter spot is, and being willing to cut an outing short without debate. Having a way out planned makes it easier for the adult to stay calm, and a calm adult helps.

## What we've accepted

Meltdowns happen. They're not the result of bad parenting. They're part of autism.

Our job isn't to prevent all meltdowns. It's to support him through them.

## When to get more help

Not every meltdown is something to manage alone at home. If your child's meltdowns involve self-injury or aggression, or are getting more frequent or intense, talk to your GP, paediatrician or psychologist. The Raising Children Network has practical guidance on [aggressive behaviour in autistic children and teenagers](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/behaviour/common-concerns/aggressive-behaviour-asd), including when to involve professionals.

## To other autism parents

You're not failing. This is hard. You're doing your best.

Your child needs understanding, not judgement. Give that to them. Give that to yourself.
