Two years ago, my wife and I were at dinner. A rare date night: kids with the grandparents, a fancy restaurant. Then my phone buzzed, and I learned that being in the room and being with someone are two different things.

## The notification that changed things

It was a production alert. I glanced at it and said, "Just one second."

That one second became fifteen minutes. By the time I looked up, she wasn't angry. She was resigned. That was worse.

"You're here but you're not here," she said.

She was right.

## The problem isn't the alert

I work in technology, and technology doesn't keep office hours. Deployments fail at midnight. Clients message on weekends. Slack never sleeps. My phone makes sure I hear about all of it, all the time.

The result is that I'm physically present and mentally divided: half with my family, half with my systems.

That's not presence. That's attendance.

I used to blame the alert. The alert wasn't the problem. Systems break, and someone has to deal with them. The problem was that I'd made myself the default answer to every buzz, at every hour, including a rare night out with my wife.

## What changed

None of this is clever. It's a set of rules simple enough that I can't argue my way around them.

### The phone drawer

When I get home, the phone goes in a drawer by the front door. It stays there until the kids are in bed.

The exception is actual emergencies, and "important" messages don't count. The bar is high on purpose.

If the phone is in a drawer, an emergency has to be loud enough to cross the room: let calls from family and the on-call person ring through, and silence everything else. It's the same rule I use for work at weekends in [The Weekend I Stopped Answering Slack](/blog/2026-01-12-weekend-work-boundaries/): if it's worth interrupting the evening, it's worth a phone call.

### Protected time

Date night is off-limits. No phones at dinner. No "quick checks."

This is the rule I'd defend hardest to other engineers: if the system can't survive two hours without me, the fault is in the system. That's a design problem and a team problem. It's not something my marriage should be absorbing. I wrote about the work side of this, and how the team adjusted, in that same weekend post.

### Honest conversations

"I'm stressed about work" is better than pretending to be present while mentally debugging.

My wife doesn't need me to be stress-free. She needs me to be honest about what's happening in my head. Saying it out loud also tends to shrink it. A problem I've named is easier to set down for the evening than one I'm quietly chewing on.

### Quality over quantity

A 30-minute walk where I'm fully present beats a whole evening where I'm half checked out on the couch.

I wrote about the same idea with my kids in [The Five-Minute Father](/blog/2026-01-24-present-moment-parenting/). Short stretches of full attention count for more than long stretches of partial attention.

## What my wife taught me

**Attention is love.** When I look at her while she talks, she feels valued. When I glance at my phone, she doesn't. There's no neutral version of that glance.

**Consistency matters more than gestures.** A daily habit of presence beats an annual grand gesture. Flowers on Valentine's Day don't make up for 364 days of distraction.

**She shouldn't be competing with my phone.** The fact that there's even a comparison means something is wrong, and it isn't her expectations.

## For the tech workers

We build systems designed to capture attention. We know the tricks: the notifications, the badges, the infinite scroll.

We should be the most resistant to them. Instead, we're often the most hooked. Knowing how the hook works doesn't stop it working on you.

If you're in the same spot, my advice is to start with one rule you can actually keep. Five at once is how you end up keeping none. Pick a place for the phone and a time it stays there. Make it physical, because willpower at 9 PM after a long day is not a strategy.

If you're on the on-call rota this week, the drawer rule bends: keep the phone where you can hear a call, but leave chat notifications off. Being reachable for a real incident is the job; watching every channel in case one turns up isn't.

Our partners and kids deserve better than what's left after Slack.

## The Valentine's Day resolution

Today I'm taking my wife to dinner. The phone stays in the car. Not in my pocket on silent. In the car.

For two hours, she gets all of me, before Slack and email have taken their share.

That's not a grand gesture. That's the minimum. But it's a start.

To my wife: thank you for being patient while I figure this out. I love you, and I'm working on showing it better.

Happy Valentine's Day.
